# ElevenLabs narration — The hodograph

Paste the text between **BEGIN NARRATION** and **END NARRATION** into an ElevenLabs project. It is written for a measured, intimate delivery of roughly four minutes.

**BEGIN NARRATION**

The hodograph is a way of seeing an orbit from a second point of view.

On one plane, a planet travels around the Sun in an ellipse. On the other, its velocity travels around a circle. Not approximately a circle. Exactly a circle, provided gravity follows the inverse-square law.

That simple change of viewpoint is the center of a remarkable lecture Richard Feynman gave at Caltech in March of 1964.

Feynman was fascinated by Newton’s geometric proof that gravity gives elliptical planetary orbits. He tried to reconstruct it in a form he could teach to freshmen, using only elementary geometry. He could not make Newton’s route work to his satisfaction. So he built another route.

The lecture was given as a special guest appearance for Caltech freshmen. It was recorded. Feynman also left Robert Leighton a few pages of handwritten notes, including sketches of the blackboard drawings. But the special lecture was not included in the final 1965 volume of The Feynman Lectures on Physics, which focused on quantum mechanics.

And that is how a lecture can be lost without disappearing completely.

The tape survived. A partial transcript survived. A few notes survived. What did not survive was the full sequence of blackboard photographs that made the argument legible. The proof lived between Feynman’s spoken words and the drawings he was making in real time.

In 1992, Caltech archivist Judith Goodstein was asked to clear Robert Leighton’s office files. There she found an unfinished folder containing material from unpublished Feynman lectures. Later, working with the tapes, transcripts, sparse sketches, and Newton’s original diagrams, David and Judith Goodstein reconstructed the planetary-motion lecture.

This instrument is not a recreation of that classroom. It is a way to reopen the relationship Feynman was teaching.

Look first at the orange construction. The planet moves around the Sun in equal intervals of time. The orange wedges are not equal in angle, because the planet speeds up near the Sun and slows down farther away. But their areas are equal. That is Kepler’s second law.

Now look at the blue construction. At each of those same instants, take the planet’s velocity as a vector. Place the vectors tip to tail. The change in velocity over each equal-time interval has the same length and turns through the same angle. A chain of equal steps that turns evenly closes into a circle.

That blue circle is the hodograph.

Its center is not generally at the velocity origin. The displacement records eccentricity. A circular orbit makes a hodograph centered on the origin. As the position orbit becomes more eccentric, the hodograph circle moves away from it.

The dashed bridge in this instrument is not a force, a rod, or a body in space. It marks one shared instant across two different mathematical spaces: position space below, velocity space above.

Try the overhead view to read the orbit as a literal plane. Try the side view to see the two constructions separate. Then travel with the Sun, the planet, or the hodograph point. The camera stays attached to that point, but the rest of the construction remains available around you.

The sound is interpretive. The continuous field follows the changing gravitational potential: it strengthens as the planet approaches the Sun. The marks arrive at exact equal-time wedge crossings and at the apsides. It is not the sound of space. It is a listening aid for the geometry.

Feynman’s wager was that a proof becomes more understandable when you can rebuild it from simple moves. The ellipse is one view. The circle is another. The hodograph lets both be true at the same time.

**END NARRATION**
