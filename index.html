FROM FRAGMENTS TO RECOVERY

A lecture preserved in fragments, then reconstructed as an argument.

The proof lived in the relation between Feynman’s voice and an evolving blackboard. A recording, sparse notes, and one lecture photograph survived; the complete blackboard sequence did not.

1964 / CALTECH

One special lecture for freshmen.

In March 1964, Feynman returned to the Caltech freshman class for a special lecture on planetary motion. He had tried to follow Newton’s geometric proof but could not get past its specialized conic-section machinery, so he built an elementary geometric proof of his own.

1964 / NOTES

A few pages hold the geometry in place.

Feynman’s sparse handwritten notes preserve the outline of the construction and some of its diagrams. They are traces of the lecture rather than a complete visual record of the proof as it unfolded at the blackboard.

1965 / OMITTED

It falls outside the canonical set.

Robert Leighton received Feynman’s handwritten notes and sketches, but the special lecture was not included in the final 1965 volume of The Feynman Lectures on Physics. Its audiotape and a few pages of notes remained; unlike the rest of the course, its complete blackboard photographs were never found.

1985 / CONTEXT

A later blackboard, not the missing one.

The Goodsteins’ account also includes this 1985 photograph of Feynman at a blackboard. It belongs to the later biographical context of the recovery, not to the surviving evidence of the 1964 lecture.

1992–94 / RECOVERED

Enough traces to reconstruct an argument.

While clearing Leighton’s office files, archivist Judith Goodstein found the unfinished material. David and Judith Goodstein later worked from the tape, transcript, Feynman’s sketches, and selected pages from Kepler and Newton to reconstruct the missing blackboard sequence.

NOW / TRANSLATED

Two spaces, one shared instant.

On the horizontal plane, Kepler’s area law marks equal times by equal swept areas. Over those matched intervals, the inverse-square pull supplies Sun-directed changes of velocity; chained on the vertical plane, they close into a circle. The orange planet and blue velocity point are one event represented twice; the dashed bridge is editorial correspondence, never a physical tether.

That is the proof move this instrument translates. Position space is the trace to be recovered; velocity space is the decisive change of language. Together, the 3D staging, camera views, bridge, and sound form another contemporary translation layer, not a feature of Feynman’s original presentation.

Historical account grounded in David and Judith Goodstein’s 1996 Caltech excerpt and its archive record. The hodograph circle predates Feynman and is associated with Hamilton (1846). Source artifacts, reconstructed argument, and this instrument’s contemporary interpretation are kept distinct.
